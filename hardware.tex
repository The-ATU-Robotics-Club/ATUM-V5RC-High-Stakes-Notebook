\subsubsection{Design Philosophy}
As a VURC team, we have the ability to manufacture parts that are created using legal raw
stock as defined by <VUR4> in the game manual. See \ref{fig:VUR4} for <VUR4>. As the first designs of the season, we will choose not to design our own gears or sprockets
initially. Having all the vex gears to choose from will allow us to save time and immediately
jump into building any subsystems that require gears or sprockets. Moreover, we will use
vex parts for the majority of the first robot designs as it will allow us to tinker with many
prototypes of ideas without having to dedicate potential days of resources into something
that could easily be replicated with vex parts that are readily available.
However, this does not mean that we will not fabricate parts of our own.

Our approach for these first bots will utilize vex parts as a rapid prototyping interface initially. As the
robot begins to take shape, several subsystems will be looked at to see if they can become more efficient via 3-D printing or
by utilizing more effective materials where vex parts may be lacking in strength or in overall design.
The reasoning behind the ’Vex-parts-first philosophy’ is simply due to our experience
with designing a custom chassis and subsystems during the pervious  competitive seasons.
For example, in previous years the majority of the chassis components and subsystems were designed with large amounts of 3-D printed components.

However, after finally getting the chassis built, if the CAD geometry conflicted with game elements or the components under-performed during the testing phase; we would hit a barrier that day progression-wise. No amount of tuning could be done to custom pieces if they needed to be raised by 1/4” for example. In the case that the team builds an identical mechanism using only Vex parts, tuning could be done immediately due to the modular 
nature of Vex parts.

\subsection{Building Two 15" Robots}
This year, we will make an interesting design choice before even starting the design. In that, we will design one 15" robot, utilize all of our research and development time to make into a quality machine, then clone it. The clone will be the 24" version. Our reasoning is simply due to more streamlined design process and being able to focus resources better. In previous years, we have always had issues with two different robots. We believe this is the best way to go this year, especially given the rule analysis.

as two tank drives joined together at 90\degree. At which point, when both run independently,

Torque scores are derived similarly to speed: assumed equivalent except for X with
analogous reasoning. The scores for simplicity are ranks. Tank is clearly the most straightforward design, with no special concerns. Next is mecanum, where the main concern is each wheel being independently-powered (and the additional width). Then H which requires an additional wheel in the center of the robot. Then X which requires each wheel be independently powered and offset by 45\degree. Finally, bogie would require two additional center wheels, each wheel be independently-powered, and an additional linkage. Strafing is simply considered as a boolean value.

\subsection{Ring Manipulation Brainstorming}
Another major subsystem involves ring manipulation. We need to develop a mechanism that, when presented a ring, can effectively control it in a manner that will allow us to score. The way the team sees it, there are at least three methods of manipulating rings: a claw, intake rollers, and intake hooks. 


Since the term 'manipulator' can be a bit vague describing a mechanism and its purpose, we are going to divide this subsection into two parts that we believe a 'ring manipulator' encompasses. Out of the three solutions proposed to manipulate rings two categories can be created: an Intake Mechanism and Scoring Mechanism. 


An intake will not necessarily score rings, but it will allow us to get a ring laying on the ground into our bot so we can later score it. Likewise, a scoring mechanism will likely not be able to intake, but it will allow us to control rings contained within our robot to score them.

\subsection{Odometry}



\subsubsection{Overview}
To accurately track our robots' movement across the field during the autonomous and driver control periods, we had to come up with an improved method of odometry. In previous seasons, our team has used the same wheel encoders with success but had trouble with the overall placement and design of the wheel module. Problems with our last season's odometer pods were that they were too bulky in size, lacked stroke length, and were too difficult to integrate into an existing chassis. We knew that we wanted to create a completely modular design that's easy to install on any configuration. To eliminate these issues, our team had to switch to a more custom and universal mechanism that could allow our tracking to work nearly perfectly. When brainstorming ideas (see \ref{fig:odom brainstorm})

for this new iteration we came up with the most important issues that the new design would address:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduce the footprint that pods take up on the underside of the chassis
    \item Increase design durability without sacrificing size constraints

    \item Decrease unnecessary slop with wheel and encoder rotations

 These improvements would allow the robots to accurately track their position relative to the field and game elements even when an unexpected event occurs such as being lifted up by another bot or game element. From our experience, it is best to design every subassembly to perform under any conditions so the drivers don't have to worry about mechanical failure. With past odometer assemblies being fragile, we knew durability would be a characteristic to focus on. Considering these goals, the team began prototyping a mechanism solely out of readily available Vex hardware. \subsection{Goal Rush Mechanism}

\subsubsection{Overview}

When brainstorming ideas in order to reach Mobile Goals, a Goal Rush mechanism we opted to design an over-center
locking clamp. This design consisted of four different components, a base, a finger, the
over-center pivot, and the connectors. The base is mounted on a c-channel which is then attached to the
drive train. The finger, which serves as the clamp, is connected to the columns on the base and
is designed to grab the mobile goal securely. The over-center pivot point is also mounted to the
columns on the base above the clamp with the connectors from one corner of the pivot
connecting to the clamp and then to the over-center pivot. The top of the pivot will connect to the
pneumatic piston connecting to the top of the pivot with a custom U-bracket.
\newline

Insert images of the original clamp design here

The reason we decided to use an over-center locking clamp was to address a common issue
with a regular pneumatic clamp, which many teams- including ours- use. The problem is that the
mobile goal can be stolen if an opposing team’s clamp is stronger. An over-center locking clamp
resolves this issue by providing security with both pneumatic and mechanical locking
mechanisms. The pneumatic piston holds the mobile goal securely, while the over-center design
mechanically locks it in place, preventing theft.
\newline

By using this design we are able to leverage the geometry and mechanical advantage to enhance
clamping force. A typical clamp has a pivot point called the center position which moves up and down
in a straight line. However, in an over-center lock, the pivot point moves slightly past the center
position, locking it into place. This action creates a force that is resisted by the mechanism’s
geometry, making it extremely difficult to unlock without intentional action. As a result, it will be
virtually impossible for another robot to steal the mobile goal from us, significantly increasing our
chances of winning the goal rush during the 30-second autonomous period.
\newline

In the first iteration of our design, the Goal Rush mech worked exactly as we envisioned it. At
this point, we had yet to decide on a way to attach it to the robot. After reviewing the design we
realized that it was beyond our specifications given for the task, such as if we did attach it to the robot it would not be within the size limits. However, with a prototype design rapid prototyping could now be done further pushing our overall design process.
\newline

In our second iteration of the locking clamp, we slimmed down the components to 0.1 inches
each and decided to print multiple pieces of each component. We would mesh the parts
together so the mechanism would have a total thickness of 0.5 inches. This design ended up
being too unstable and lacked rigidity, even with 100 percent infill of ABS filament. We quickly realized
that the excessive space between each component was the cause of its fragility. So we decided
to utilize that unused space by filling it with plastic, making the mechanism much stronger while
still staying within 0.5 inches. This ended up as our final product.
